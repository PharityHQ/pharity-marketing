Adding peptide therapy to your practice

Peptide therapy is the category clinics ask about most and the one with the most noise around it. This is the operational picture: what you need in place, how the supply side works, and what it is honest to say to a patient.

By the Pharity team Reviewed October 3, 2026 General information, not legal advice

In one paragraph. Peptides are prescribed by a licensed clinician for a named patient and compounded by a licensed pharmacy against that prescription. They are not a product you stock, not something a practice can sell without a prescriber, and not FDA-approved finished drugs. If you have a prescriber and the capacity to follow patients, the operational lift is smaller than most people expect. If you do not, no amount of sourcing solves it.

What is actually being prescribed

In this context a peptide preparation is a compounded, non-controlled preparation made by a 503A pharmacy for an identified patient against a specific prescription, or by a 503B outsourcing facility operating under FDA registration.

That matters more than it sounds. A compounded preparation is not an FDA-approved drug product: it has not been reviewed by FDA for safety, effectiveness or quality. That is not a loophole, it is the legal category, and it belongs in how your practice discusses it with a patient rather than in the small print.

What a practice needs before offering it

  • A clinician licensed in the state the patient is in, prescribing on their own judgment after an evaluation that is capable of concluding no.
  • A real provider-patient relationship, not an intake form.
  • Follow-up capacity. These are programs with titration and monitoring, not single visits.
  • A pharmacy licensed to ship into that patient's state.

Notably absent from that list: a DEA registration. These preparations are non-controlled, so this category carries none of the controlled-substance prescribing burden.

How the supply side actually works

You do not hold inventory. The prescription goes to a compounding pharmacy, which prepares it for that patient and ships directly to them. Nothing sits on your shelf, nothing expires, and your practice never takes possession of medication.

The practical friction is that pharmacies are licensed state by state and priced individually, so the pharmacy that can serve a patient in one state may not be licensed for the next, and the same preparation can differ in price between two pharmacies that are both perfectly good. Most practices discover this the first time a patient moves.

What to ask a compounding pharmacy

  • Which states are you licensed to ship into, and when does each licence expire?
  • 503A or 503B, and which standards do you work to: USP 795, 797, 800?
  • What is your actual lead time, measured rather than advertised?
  • What is your price for this preparation, and what is the all-in cost to me once any platform fee is included?

That last question is the one most often answered incompletely. A price that excludes the fee you will actually be charged is not a comparable number.

The honest limits

We are a routing and ordering platform, so there are things this page should not do. It will not tell you that a peptide works, that it suits a given patient, or what to prescribe. Those are clinical questions and they belong to the clinician who evaluated that person.

What we can say is operational: the category is non-controlled, the supply runs through licensed compounding pharmacies, your practice holds no inventory, and the decision stays entirely with your prescriber.

Who should not add it

  • A business with no prescriber. That is a different structure entirely, and the brands route is the right one.
  • A practice that cannot follow patients over months.
  • Anyone whose plan depends on a high prescribe rate. If a clinician declining is a problem for the model, the model is wrong.

General information rather than clinical or legal advice. What is appropriate for a patient is a matter for the treating clinician, and scope of practice rules vary by state.

Common questions

Are peptides legal to prescribe?
A licensed clinician may prescribe a compounded preparation for a patient they have evaluated, and a licensed pharmacy may compound it against that prescription. Compounded preparations are not FDA-approved drug products, which is a distinction that belongs in the patient conversation.
Do I need a DEA registration for peptide therapy?
Not for this category. These are non-controlled compounded preparations, so the controlled-substance prescribing and electronic-prescribing requirements do not apply.
Do I have to hold inventory?
No. The pharmacy compounds for the individual patient and ships directly to them. Your practice never takes possession of the medication and has nothing to stock or expire.
Can a gym or supplement brand offer peptide therapy?
Not directly. A business with no prescriber cannot prescribe, choose a medication or dispense. It can market a program alongside an independent professional entity that supplies the clinicians, which is a different structure.
How do I compare pharmacies on price?
Ask for the all-in number, not the preparation price alone. A quote that excludes the platform fee you will actually be charged is not comparable to one that includes it.
What should I ask about a pharmacy licence?
Which states it is licensed to ship into and when each licence expires. A lapsed licence in your patient state stops that order, whatever the rest of the relationship looks like.
