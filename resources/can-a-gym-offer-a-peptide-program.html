Can a gym offer a peptide program?

Short answer: not on its own. A gym can market a program and own the member relationship, but it cannot prescribe, choose a medication or dispense one. Here is who does, and how the two fit together.

By the Pharity team Reviewed October 3, 2026 General information, not legal advice

The direct answer. A gym cannot prescribe a prescription medication, cannot decide which one a member should receive, and cannot hold or hand over medication. Those are acts reserved to licensed clinicians and licensed pharmacies. A gym can market a program, own the member relationship, and be paid for its own services, and that is a real business rather than a consolation prize.

Why the answer is not "get a licence"

The usual follow-up is whether the gym can obtain some licence of its own. It cannot, because the licences in question are personal to clinicians and institutional to pharmacies. A prescriber is licensed as an individual by a state board. A pharmacy is licensed as a facility. Neither is a permission a fitness business applies for.

Most states also restrict the corporate practice of medicine, which in plain terms means a lay company cannot own a medical practice or direct clinical judgment. That is why the lawful structures in this space all put the clinicians inside a professional entity owned by a physician, separate from the marketing business.

What a gym may lawfully do

  • Market a program to its own members, truthfully.
  • Own the member relationship, the brand and the experience.
  • Sell its own services: coaching, programming, an app, onboarding, check-ins, accountability and support.
  • Set its own price for those services, as a flat price.
  • Hand a member off to a licensed clinician who decides independently.

What it may not do, and these are absolute

  • Diagnose. Including informally, on the gym floor.
  • Prescribe or recommend. This includes naming a specific medication as something the gym offers or that a member should ask for.
  • Choose the medication, the dose or the protocol.
  • Dispense, handle or take title to medication. Receiving a drug that is not for your own use is distribution, and distribution is licensed.

Can a gym offer semaglutide, tirzepatide or testosterone?

No, and the question is worth answering directly because it is the one people actually type. Those are prescription medications. Whether any of them is appropriate for a particular person is a clinical decision made by a licensed clinician after an evaluation, and it is not a product a gym stocks or advertises.

What a gym can do is run a program in which members have access to a consultation with a licensed clinician, who may prescribe if they judge it appropriate. The difference between those two sentences is the whole legal distinction, and it has to survive into the marketing copy, not just the contract.

The money rule people get wrong

A gym may not earn a margin on a medication, and its own price must not move with what a member is prescribed. A program price that is higher for members who receive a prescription converts a service fee into a share of a prescription, which is the arrangement every fee-splitting and anti-kickback rule in this area exists to prevent.

The workable shape is simple: a flat price for the gym's own program, charged whether or not anything is ever prescribed, for things the gym actually delivers.

What a compliant structure looks like

Three independent parties, each doing only what it is licensed to do.

  • The gym markets to its own members and runs its own program at its own flat price.
  • A professional entity owned by a physician supplies the clinicians, who evaluate and decide independently.
  • A licensed pharmacy dispenses and ships to the member directly.

Nobody in that chain is paid per prescription by the gym, and the gym never touches the medication.

The thing to settle before you start

If a business is already selling something in this category without a clinician, that has to be wound down rather than run alongside a compliant program. Operating both under one brand is the fastest way to turn a remediable problem into an unfixable one, and it is the single most common reason an otherwise good applicant is declined.

This article is general information about how these arrangements are usually structured. It is not legal advice, and a business considering one should take its own counsel on its own facts.

Common questions

Can a gym hire a doctor and offer it that way?
Hiring a clinician does not by itself solve it. Many states restrict the corporate practice of medicine, which prevents a lay company from owning a medical practice or directing clinical judgment. The common lawful structure places clinicians inside a professional entity owned by a physician, kept separate from the marketing business.
Can a gym keep a margin on the medication?
No. A gym may not price, collect, keep, discount, waive or share any part of a prescription charge, and its own program price must not vary with what a member is prescribed. The gym is paid a flat price for its own services.
Can a gym pay trainers a commission for signing members up?
No. Staff incentives tied to enrolments, consultations or prescriptions are prohibited, and in some states that kind of incentive has criminal statutes behind it.
Does a gym need its own pharmacy licence?
No, and it should not seek one. The gym never receives, holds or ships medication. A licensed pharmacy dispenses and ships directly to the member.
What can a gym actually charge for?
Its own program: coaching, programming, the app, onboarding, adherence support and the member experience. Those are real services the gym delivers, and they are what the flat program price pays for.
